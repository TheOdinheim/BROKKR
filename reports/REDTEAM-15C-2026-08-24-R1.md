## 1. Summary

**~11 attack vectors across 3 categories. 7 runtime tests pass. 0 critical, 0 high.** Three findings (F-29 … F-31): one LOW (a replay window opened by the F-13 memory bound), two INFO (SAGA end-truncation, content-blind auditing). Plus the campaign's **terminal observation**: every residual across 15A–15C roots in a single trust assumption — the DAP.

| Category | Result |
|---|---|
| G1 — governance layer | The DAP is the root of trust the spine cannot constrain; there is no cross-session scope monotonicity; the audit trail records but does not prevent authorized actions — all **by design**, all named |
| G2 — persistent/covert | Content-based anomaly detection is a detector concern, not the spine's; env-probe and tool output are ungoverned (F-31); the F-13 cap opens a bounded replay window (F-29); SAGA end-truncation needs an external witness (F-30) |
| Combined campaign | recon → enumerate → exploit → persist → anti-forensics: the attacker **succeeds within authority and the malicious content is invisible in the audit** — but every *action* is recorded and attributable |

**The low-noise result, and the campaign's thesis:** BROKKR is an **authorization** system, not a content-prevention system. It governs *whether an actor may act*, structurally and unforgeably. It does **not** govern what a legitimately-authorized tool *does with* its authority — the content it writes, the data it reads, the bytes it emits. A patient attacker who operates strictly inside granted authority is bounded not by the spine but by the DAP's scoping and by out-of-band controls (OS sandboxing, an audit witness, content monitoring). The spine makes that attacker **fully attributable** — it cannot make them impossible. That boundary is exactly where the architecture says it is (§13: the over-broad-root-scope residual, the tool-content residual), and 15C confirms it from the patient attacker's side.

## 3. Findings (F-29 … F-31)

### F-29 (LOW) — the F-13 nonce-ledger cap opens a bounded replay window
**Where:** `Orchestrator::check_guards` — `MAX_NONCE_ENTRIES = 10_000`; at capacity the smallest-expiry `(principal, nonce)` entry is evicted before a new insert (14-FIX F-13).
**What:** F-13 bounded the ledger's memory. The tradeoff, surfaced by the patient attacker: driving `MAX_NONCE_ENTRIES` distinct nonces (all with expiry ≥ a target's) evicts the target's entry, after which the target chain — **still within its own expiry** — is replayed and admitted by the guard. The replay protection for any single nonce is therefore finite in the face of nonce-space exhaustion.
**Why LOW:** (a) realizability depends on driving 10⁴ distinct-nonce hops with controlled expiries, which in a real deployment means the **DAP** (the chain signer) is generating them — the same root trust as everything else; (b) SINDRI's Signal-2 freshness still denies a chain past its own expiry, so the window is bounded by the target's expiry (which OQGF-M-14 bounds to the credential lifetime); (c) the alternative (unbounded ledger) is the memory-exhaustion DoS F-13 fixed. It is a genuine tradeoff worth naming. **Recommendation:** if replay-window hardening is wanted, prefer evicting by *age of insertion* over *smallest expiry*, or raise the cap with a memory ceiling — but the honest fix is an external, bounded-lifetime nonce store, out of scope for the driver layer.

### F-30 (INFO) — SAGA end-truncation is undetectable by `verify_chain` alone
**Where:** `brokkr-audit::Saga::verify_chain` — verifies each present record's `prev` link and signature.
**What:** `verify_chain` proves that *what is present* was not altered and is correctly linked, but it has no notion of *expected length*. Removing the most-recent k records yields a valid shorter chain (record 0 → genesis intact, links 0..n-k intact), which verifies `Intact`. An attacker with write access to a **file-backed** SAGA could drop the latest records — the most incriminating ones — invisibly. 14A confirmed a *middle* deletion is caught (the following record's `prev` mismatches); **end-truncation is the distinct, uncaught case.**
**Why INFO:** SAGA is **in-memory** in the current build — there is no on-disk chain to truncate, so this is not realizable today. It is a property of any append-only log: detecting *removal from the end* requires an external witness (a monotonic sequence counter checkpointed elsewhere, cross-jurisdictional replication per OQGF-R-5, or a third-party notary). Named as a deployment requirement. **Recommendation:** for a persisted SAGA, checkpoint the head sequence number to an independent store (or the OQGF-R-5 cross-jurisdictional replica, already a High-Assurance requirement) so a shortened chain is detectable.

### F-31 (INFO) — content-blind auditing: the action is recorded, its effect is not
**Where:** the audit path — `ProposalRecord` records the model, the input derivative, the action `detail`, and the sanitized rationale; the reconciliation `Observation::Hop` records the action. **Nothing records the tool's `ToolOutcome.output` or the bytes a tool writes.**
**What:** SAGA answers "what action was authorized and taken" (wrote to `/tmp/config.yaml`), never "what was the content" (what bytes went into it) or "what did the tool emit" (the covert-channel output, F-27). A fully-authorized action's *effect* is outside the audit trail. This is the audit-side face of the spine's governing principle: it governs *authority*, not *content*.
**Why INFO:** consistent and by design — the spine is an authorization system (§3 thesis), and content auditing is a different capability (tool-level logging, filesystem monitoring, DLP on outputs). Named because a patient attacker relies on exactly this gap for anti-forensics. **Recommendation:** where content accountability is required, add tool-level content logging or route tool outputs that cross a classified boundary through HÚÐ egress; the spine's audit is deliberately authority-scoped.

## 4. Confirmed-sound against the patient attacker (the grey-hat "PASS" list)

- **Every action is attributable.** The attacker succeeds only within granted authority, and every authorization and proposal is recorded, signed, and hash-chained — they cannot act *unrecorded* (only, per F-31, without the *content* recorded).
- **The replay window is bounded by expiry** (F-29): SINDRI Signal-2 freshness still denies a chain past its own OQGF-M-14 expiry; exhaustion buys a window, not a permanent bypass.
- **Truncation is not tampering** (F-30): the present chain is always integrity-verified; only *absence* is invisible, and only against a persisted store — a witness requirement, already anticipated by OQGF-R-5.
- **The model never gains authority** through any of this: enumeration, timing, covert output, and env-probing are all *reconnaissance and exfiltration by a legitimately-authorized tool*, never a path to an unauthorized action.

## 5. The campaign's terminal observation — the DAP

Across 15A–15C, every residual converges on one point: **the DAP is the root of trust, and the spine is designed to make the DAP accountable, not to constrain a malicious one.** The DAP scopes the Root Intent (over-broad scope → AMD-001/M-13 residual), owns the Self Set and genome (mislabel → AMD-002 residual), signs SAGA (F-15A 1.3), configures the guards (15A 2.4), and holds the keys (OQGF-R-6, PARTIAL). This is not a defect — it is the deliberate shape of "360-degree accountability rooted in the organism, not the pathogen" (OQGF Part B). A framework can make the accountable party *named, signed, recorded, and reviewable*; it cannot make them *trustworthy*. Every prior amendment names this same residual shape (AMD-001 over-broad root, AMD-002 Self Set, AMD-006 risk-acceptance quality, AMD-008 disposition quality). 15C confirms it is the floor: **the patient attacker who compromises or coerces the DAP defeats the system, and no structural control below the DAP can prevent it** — which is why the DAP is a named natural person, reviewed, and never an entity.

## 6. Scope honesty — and the close of the grey-hat campaign

Low-noise grey hat, the patient layer. The three findings are a LOW tradeoff (F-29, the price of the F-13 memory bound), and two INFO deployment/design boundaries (F-30 truncation needs a witness; F-31 auditing is authority-scoped, not content-scoped) — none an authorization bypass, all realizable only *within* granted authority or *outside* the current (in-memory, single-node) build. Not covered, and honestly out of scope for a hermetic test: a live persisted-SAGA deployment with an external witness (the F-30 mitigation), OS-level tool sandboxing (the F-27/F-28/F-31 mitigations), and the human-factors testing of DAP compromise (§5 — a red-team of the *organization*, not the code).

**Across the whole grey-hat campaign (15A–15C): ~36 attack vectors, 24 runtime tests, 0 critical, 0 high, 0 authorization bypasses.** Ten findings (F-22 … F-31): one LOW code gap the earlier phases missed and its enumeration impact (F-22/F-26), one LOW tradeoff (F-29), and seven INFO trust/deployment boundaries (F-23, F-24, F-25, F-27, F-28, F-30, F-31). The consistent result: **partial knowledge lets a patient attacker map the system, time it, and operate within authority, but the structural spine yields no path to unauthorized action — and the residuals are the ones the architecture already names, rooted in the DAP.**
